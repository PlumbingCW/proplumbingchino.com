How to Install a Toilet Flush Valve in Chino, CA

A flush valve is the big drain at the bottom of the tank. When it wears out or cracks, no flapper will seal it. Replacing it is a steady afternoon job.

The flush valve sits at the bottom of the toilet tank and opens to let water drop into the bowl. A rubber flapper or seal closes it between flushes. Over the years the flapper hardens and the seat on the valve itself can pit or crack. When that happens, water leaks slowly into the bowl, the fill valve cycles on its own and your water bill creeps up.

Swapping only the flapper is the first fix to try, and it costs little. If a new flapper does not stop the leak, the valve seat is the problem, and the whole flush valve needs replacing. That means taking the tank off the bowl, which is more work but well within reach for most homeowners.

Confirm the leak first

Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait fifteen minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper or its seat is leaking. If the water level in the tank is above the overflow tube, you have a fill valve problem instead. A running toilet that stops when you jiggle the handle often has a bad chain or flapper, not a bad valve.

Run a finger along the seat where the flapper lands. A rough, pitted or cracked seat is the reason to replace the whole valve. A smooth seat with a worn flapper only needs a new flapper.

Gather parts and tools

You need a flush valve kit that matches your toilet, which means checking whether the opening is two or three inches and whether the tank bolts are the two or three bolt type. You also need a tank to bowl gasket, new tank bolts and washers, a wrench, a sponge, a bucket and a flat screwdriver. Many universal kits cover the common sizes, but check the fit against your model.

Remove the tank

Close the supply valve at the wall and flush to empty the tank. Sponge out the rest. Disconnect the supply line at the fill valve with a wrench, with a bucket below. Remove the nuts on the bolts under the tank, then lift the tank straight up and set it on a towel.

Unscrew the large plastic spud nut under the tank that holds the old flush valve in place. Lift out the valve, and clean the tank bottom so the new gasket seals against a smooth surface.

Install and reassemble

Set the new valve in the tank with its rubber gasket seated, then thread the spud nut on from below. Snug it by hand and give it a small turn with a wrench. Porcelain cracks if you overtighten. Place the new tank to bowl gasket on the bowl opening, set the tank down level, insert the bolts with washers and tighten them evenly in alternating turns.

Reconnect the supply line, open the valve slowly and let the tank fill. Check for drips at the spud nut, the bolts and the supply connection. Flush several times and look again after an hour.

When a plumber is the better call

If the tank or bowl is cracked, if the bolts are rusted solid or if the toilet is an older model with hard to find parts, replacing the toilet may make more sense than repairing it. A toilet that rocks at the floor may have a bad wax seal. Our toilet repair page covers what a visit includes, and plumbing installation covers a full replacement.

Check the job a day later

Dry the floor and the outside of the tank with a towel, then lay a sheet of toilet paper against each joint. Any dampness shows up on the paper the next morning. Look at the supply connection and the bolt heads in particular. A slow weep at a bolt washer is common right after a reinstall and usually stops with a small, even snug.
